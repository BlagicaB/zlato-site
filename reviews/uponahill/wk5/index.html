Elizabeth Moen didn't play her first open mic until she was 20

Same post as last week, with the price added and a sharper deadline — her show is now three weeks from tomorrow. Reruns work when the first run reached mostly non-followers, which ours did: 87% of everything we've reached has been new to us, so the overlap between run one and run two is small.

Facebook

Elizabeth Moen opens Up On A Hill three weeks from tomorrow — and she got a very late start. She taught herself guitar as a teenager in small-town Iowa but didn't play her first open mic until she was 20, as a French and Spanish major at the University of Iowa, at a bar in Iowa City called The Mill. She'd just written her first song. Nine years later she's toured Europe singing in Kevin Morby's band, shared stages with Brittany Howard, Hozier and Ani DiFranco, and had NPR say she "has remarkable control over her voice that reflects the emotional candor in her lyrics." Her new record was produced by Spencer Tweedy. She's releasing it herself. She describes what she does as indie rock grit through the warmth of Midwestern folk. In a tent on a hill at sunset, with two hundred people, that's going to be something. September 2. Gates at 4. General admission $35, reserved seating $75. uponahill.org

Wed · Aug 12 Artist · Fun fact Facebook + Instagram

Chicago had a country music radio show before Nashville did

Big Sadie's turn. The hook isn't the band — it's a piece of history most people have never heard, and Big Sadie are the band who get called to talk about it. It gives a Michigan and Chicago audience a reason to feel some ownership over a roots band, which is exactly the reframe we want for September 16.

Facebook

Here's something most people don't know: Chicago had a country music radio show before Nashville did. The WLS Barn Dance was on the air before the Grand Ole Opry ever started. So when WTTW needed a band to talk about that history on Chicago Tonight — when Ken Burns's country music documentary aired — they called Big Sadie. Big Sadie play Up On A Hill on September 16. They're a Chicago band, built around Elise Bergman on upright bass and Collin Moore on guitar, a married pair who've been playing music together since 2005 — she came up through Chicago's blues and folk scene, he grew up in East Tennessee learning from his mother. One of them teaches fiddle at the Old Town School of Folk Music. Bloodshot Records, the label that more or less defined Chicago roots music, put them on a compilation called "Too Late to Pray: Defiant Chicago Roots." What they do is old and new at once: upright bass, close harmony, original country songs that sound like they've been around a hundred years. If you've ever wanted to hear voices lock together like that in a tent on a hill at sunset — this is your night. September 16. General admission $35, reserved seating $75. uponahill.org

Thu · Aug 13 The DJ · All three nights Facebook + Instagram

Most festivals play a playlist. We have a jazz drummer with a record bag.

Quin Kirchner has been mentioned in nearly every post and never given one of his own — and he's the only person on the bill who plays all three nights, so a post about him sells every date at once. It also quietly answers "what happens between 4:30 and 6," which is ninety minutes people currently can't picture.

Facebook

Every night on the hill starts the same way: gates at 4, and at 4:30, Quin Kirchner starts playing records. Not a playlist. Records — his own, out of a bag, chosen for that hour and that light. He describes what he plays as moody grooves, dusty funk, cosmic throwbacks and spaced-out oddities, and if you've been to The Whistler in Chicago on the right night, you've heard him do it. The rest of his résumé is not what you'd expect from someone warming up a crowd. He studied Afro-Cuban percussion in Havana. He won a scholarship to the University of New Orleans at 18 and studied under Jason Marsalis and Terence Blanchard. The Chicago Reader called his first album "the first great jazz album of 2018." Pitchfork gave the follow-up an 8.0. He's played with Jeff Parker, Rob Mazurek and Patricia Barber, in more than twenty countries. He's also played drums in Erik Hall's bands, which is how he ended up here. Erik curated this festival, and he brought the person he'd want to hear. So the ninety minutes before the headliner isn't filler. It's the part where you get a drink, find your people, and let someone very good set the evening up. All three nights — September 2, 16 and 30. General admission $35, reserved seating $75. uponahill.org

Sat · Aug 15 Artist · Fun fact Facebook + Instagram

He named his restaurant after a Duke Ellington song

Liam Kazar is our best-performing subject by a wide margin — the July 26 post did 2,800 views on Instagram against 54 on Facebook, on identical copy. The Isfahan story is the most repostable thing in the whole campaign: specific, warm, and with a double meaning most people will want to tell someone about.

Facebook

When the pandemic took away both his touring income and his bartending job, Liam Kazar started cooking his mother's Armenian recipes for a living. He called it Isfahan — after a city in Iran with one of the oldest Armenian quarters in the world, dating back to the 17th century. And, as he'll tell you, after his favorite Duke Ellington tune. Both at once. He says he grew up watching Food Network instead of cartoons. The menu follows his family's route out of Armenia, through Syria, Lebanon and France, to America. The music has been going just as long. He played SXSW at seventeen. He's spent more than a decade in Jeff Tweedy's band, and backed Steve Gunn, Chance the Rapper and Daniel Johnston along the way. His sister is Sima Cunningham of OHMME. His latest record, "Pilot Light," was made with Sam Evian in the Catskills. Jeff Tweedy on him: "Whenever I hear one of his songs for the first time I almost immediately start thinking… I love this song." Chicago-born, Brooklyn-based, and closing out our first year on September 30. General admission $35, reserved seating $75. uponahill.org
